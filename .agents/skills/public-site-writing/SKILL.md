# Public-site writing

Write like someone who knows the grid and wants a friend to have a go at running one. Give the reader enough to make the next decision. Let the game supply the proof.

## Show what happens

- Open with the situation the reader came for. On a scenario page, name the place and what makes running its grid tricky. On a classroom page, start with what students do. An invite should tell the recipient what they're joining.
- Put the person doing the work in the sentence. Use “you” for player choices and name what the player controls. “You set the price” is easier to picture than “pricing decisions are part of the experience.”
- When a mechanic needs explaining, show one cause and its effect. “Solar stops producing at night. What will power the city until morning?” gives the reader a decision. A question earns its place when the answer matters to the reader. A related link rarely needs a question to introduce it.
- Bring in a technical term, unit, or number when it changes the reader's understanding of the choice. Explain a deadline by saying what has to be ready by then. When the effect can be expressed as one accurate figure, spare the reader the intermediate arithmetic. Preserve the units, conditions, and uncertainty that make it true.

## Keep it conversational

- Use everyday verbs and natural contractions. Mix a short statement with a longer sentence when the consequence needs room. Read both aloud. If the second repeats the first in grander terms, cut it.
- Let one telling detail carry the interest. A wind farm on a calm day raises a real problem. A promise of an immersive journey through complex challenges gives the reader little to work with. Dry humor can come from the situation itself. Give instructions, errors, and privacy disclosures a straight answer.
- Cut lines that explain a familiar word, announce what the next paragraph covers, or sell a feature the example has already demonstrated. Keep a qualifier when removing it would change a rule, a comparison, or a privacy promise. Preserve exact interface names when the reader needs to find a control.

## Name the next action

- Use the verb the visitor would use and the thing they're here for. Todd's example is **Learn how to play** in place of **See how it plays**. Follow the link before choosing its label. Starting a game, opening a guide, and comparing simulators are different actions.
- For a related link, the topic alone often works. “Compare energy simulators” can stand on its own. Keep the recognizable subject when shortening navigation for a phone.
- Read each heading with its first sentence and each link without the preceding sentence. The heading should orient the reader, the sentence should add information, and the link should tell them where they'll land.
