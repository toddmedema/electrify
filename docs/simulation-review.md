# Simulation review: credible lessons without operator training

Reviewed against `24d7dc1` (2026-09-08). This is a code and model review, not a classroom usability study or a certification of engineering accuracy. The audience is a high-school student learning how supply, reliability, cost, and emissions interact.

The strongest parts of the model are its real-reducer simulator, deterministic weather and replay paths, construction delays, finite storage, and explicit fuel and carbon costs. Those support understandable tradeoffs. The highest-priority problems are rules that teach a false causal relationship or violate energy conservation. More equations would not fix the educational problem.

## Corrections selected for this PR

### 1. A single utility controls its weather — high priority

**Evidence:** `src/data/Weather.tsx`, `climateShift` and `applyClimateForcing`, previously mapped cumulative player emissions to up to 3°C warming and 35% greater deviations in temperature, wind, and irradiance. At 115 million tonnes, the temperature bias was about 1.90°C. This modified recorded weather as well as future weather.

**Why it matters:** It teaches students that their local plant choices quickly determine local weather. IPCC's best estimate is 0.45°C of global warming per **1,000 billion tonnes of CO2**, not per utility-scale emissions. The old game factor is many orders of magnitude too large. CO2-equivalent accounting is also not directly interchangeable with cumulative CO2 for a temperature calculation. See [IPCC AR6, D.1.1](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/summary-for-policymakers/).

**Change:** Remove player-emissions forcing from weather. Keep recorded and seeded weather, authored emergencies, emissions totals, scores, and carbon fees. This removes a hidden feedback loop rather than replacing it with a new climate simulator. It does not mean emissions have no real climate impact, nor does it make the game's weather generator a global climate projection.

### 2. Storage can consume and sell the same electricity — high priority

**Evidence:** `src/reducers/Game.tsx`, `updateSupplyFacilitiesFinances`, previously filled storage from a surplus before accounting for charging efficiency. It then increased `charge` by the stored power divided by efficiency, but passed gross supply to transmission clearing. Dispatch requests for generators ahead of batteries also omitted already-produced supply, and paused batteries still requested charging.

**Concrete example:** A 10 MW surplus at 80% efficiency can add at most 8 MW to stored energy. Storing 10 MW requires 12.5 MW from the grid. That electricity cannot also be exported or counted as available to customers.

**Change:** Limit grid-side charging to available surplus and rated power, apply efficiency once, subtract charging before market clearing, and request only remaining charging demand from subsequent generators. Preserve the existing storage display convention and add regression coverage for multiple plants, storage units, and paused units. Energy accounting is essential realism; it should happen automatically.

QA also identified the related plant-revenue allocation problem: dividing revenue by net supply after charging can credit plants with more than the company earned. Allocate revenue against gross local output plus imports instead, including export proceeds and leaving the imported share outside local plant lifetime totals.

The broader tutorial tests exposed a floating-point edge case in imports: supply could end up about 0.00000006 W below demand despite fully covering the deficit. Market clearing now returns demand exactly when imports cover it; capped imports still leave a real shortage. No tutorial fleet or victory threshold was relaxed.

### 3. Oil's carbon factor is too low — high priority

**Evidence:** `src/Constants.tsx`, `FUELS.Oil`, used 20.31 kg per million Btu. The facility is modeled as an oil reciprocating engine. EIA gives **74.14 kg CO2 per million Btu** for distillate oil in its [electric-power emissions table](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/annual/table.php?t=epa_a_03.html).

**Why it matters:** The old factor understated oil emissions and carbon charges by about 3.65 times, distorting plant comparisons. The blanket comment calling all fuel factors lifecycle emissions was also misleading: the table mixes different accounting assumptions, and zero operational emissions are not zero lifecycle emissions.

**Change:** Correct the oil conversion and describe the boundary honestly. This does not claim the other factors form a complete, harmonized lifecycle inventory; that remains separate work.

### 4. Solar temperature losses are exaggerated and output is unbounded — medium priority

**Evidence:** `src/helpers/Energy.tsx`, `getSolarOutputFactor`, previously reduced power by 1% per degree of **ambient** temperature above 10°C. At 40°C air temperature it removed 30%, regardless of irradiance-driven panel heating. The helper could also return negative output or more than nameplate at extreme inputs.

**Change:** Use one simple cell-temperature approximation, a rounded 0.4%/°C power coefficient referenced to 25°C cell temperature, and bound output to the game's 0–100% rating. [PVWatts documentation](https://samrepo.nrelcloud.org/help/pvwatts.html) describes approximately 45°C nominal operating cell temperature and a 0.37%/°C coefficient for standard modules. The game's rounded approximation is intentionally less detailed than PVWatts; it is not a calibrated model of panel mounting, wind cooling, or inverter sizing.

## What to simplify for students

One further emissions boundary needs attention: `Game.tsx` calculates emissions from local generators, while imports add a financial expense without a corresponding emissions total. Buying electricity can therefore move pollution outside the player's recorded total. A future change should distinguish local plant emissions from emissions associated with purchased electricity before comparing those strategies as equally clean; it needs an explicit neighboring-generation assumption, not an invented zero-carbon import factor.

Use three questions for each decision: **Will it keep the lights on? Can we afford it? What will it emit?** The [student guide](simulation-student-guide.md) gives a short path through those questions without requiring loan formulas, dispatch optimization, or hydrology.

Keep weather, construction delays, charging losses and carbon costs because they change the answer to those questions. Keep their calculations automatic. Further interaction changes should be tested with students: code complexity alone is not evidence that a screen is confusing.

## Validation and limits

Two existing balance checks needed follow-through. Austin's demand scale changes from 7.61 to 7.7 after removing artificial warming, keeping annual energy and peak demand within the original published-target tolerances. The automated nuclear-shutdown CEO example replaces its 400 MW oil build with gas: the old example depended on understated oil carbon charges. Its scenario, ten-decision requirement, completion assertion, and omitted-choice failure assertions remain unchanged.

Save version 5 and replay version 7 deliberately reject earlier versions: their stored forecasts, financial results, and action outcomes were calculated with different physics. Existing files are not deleted, but continuing those runs requires the older game version. This compatibility break prevents silently mixing old and corrected simulation results.

The PR should verify energy conservation and trade accounting, output bounds, oil emissions and carbon costs, deterministic weather, and save/replay compatibility. Run the complete `npm run check` and production build, and have a separate QA agent review the implementation. Scenario invariant success means the accounting is lawful; it does **not** prove that every scenario is balanced or every student will succeed. Record actual results in the PR rather than treating this checklist as evidence of passing tests.
