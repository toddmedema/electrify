For teachers and facilitators

Give your class a grid to run

What keeps the lights on when the sun goes down? Let students find out by running a grid. In Electrify’s first three lessons, they watch supply and demand, build power plants, and try storing energy for later.

Use the lesson planStudent worksheet

What students will practice

  • Read a supply-and-demand chart and explain why electricity has to be available when it is needed.
  • Compare generation choices using cost, construction time, and when a plant can produce power.
  • Explain how storage moves energy from one time to another, and why it cannot supply power indefinitely.
  • Support a decision with an observation from the game.

Plan for about 30–45 minutes with learners who are comfortable reading simple charts. We haven’t piloted this lesson in a classroom yet, so try it yourself and adjust the pace and vocabulary for your group.

Before class

Use one computer, tablet, or phone per pair, or project the game and make decisions together. Electrify is free and works on computers, tablets, and phones. Students can play without creating accounts. Check that the game loads on your school’s network before the lesson.

Open Electrify and choose Play. A new player goes straight into Electricity, the first tutorial. On a device used before, choose Start a new game and find the lessons under Learn the basics; use View all to find an earlier lesson.

Have paper ready, or use your browser’s Print command (Ctrl+P or Command+P) to print the student worksheet on this page. Ask partners to take turns at the controls between lessons.

Introduce three terms: demand is the power customers need; generation is power produced by plants; storage saves energy for later. MW measures power at a moment; MWh measures an amount of energy.

A lesson in four steps

1. Predict before playing · 5 minutes

Ask: “Could a city run on solar power all day and night?” Have each pair write a prediction and name something they would need to find out. Keep these predictions for the discussion at the end.

2. Follow the first two lessons · 10–15 minutes

Play Electricity and watch the supply-and-demand chart as daylight fades. Ask students to record which plant is still supplying power in the evening.

Continue to Generators. Before buying, compare two options in Build: what do they cost, how long will construction take, and when can they produce power? Follow the lesson to order two different generator types. Record a reason for each choice.

3. Try storing energy · 10–15 minutes

In Storage, follow the prompts to open Build → Storage, review a purchase, and watch the storage bars. In the final challenge, put generation above storage in the facility order and watch spare electricity charge it before the evening peak.

The final challenge restarts with a smaller coal plant and the original storage; the practice purchase is cleared. Ask students to explain when storage charges, when it supplies power, and what would happen if it emptied too soon.

4. Compare explanations · 5–10 minutes

Return to the opening prediction. Each pair shares one observation that changed or supported its answer and one question the game did not settle. If the group needs more time, stop after Generators and use Storage in a second session.

Student worksheet: keep the lights on

Play Electrify’s Electricity, Generators, and Storage lessons at electrifygame.com. Record what you observe, then use it to explain your choices. Names: ____________________   Date: __________

  1. Make a prediction. Could a city run on solar power all day and night? What else might it need?
  2. Electricity: What happens to solar output as daylight fades? Which plant still supplies power? Describe what you saw on the chart.
  3. Generators: Name two options you compared. Record a difference in cost or construction time, and explain why you chose one. What might make the other useful?
  4. Storage: When did your storage charge, and when did it supply electricity? Where did that energy come from? What would happen if it ran out before demand fell?
  5. Revisit your prediction. What would you add to a grid with lots of solar power to cover the evening? Use an observation from the game and name one tradeoff.
  6. Go beyond the game. What information would you need before recommending this plan for a real town?

Notes for the discussion

Ask students to explain their choices. Students should notice that solar output falls as daylight fades, that construction takes time, and that storage must charge before it can help. Different generator choices can be reasonable if students explain the costs and when each plant would help.

Ask what the game leaves out. Keeping this game’s lights on does not prove a real city would be reliable all year. Electrify uses one representative day per month and simplifies the electricity system. Ask what more students would want to know about weather, demand, local infrastructure, and costs.

Make emissions a follow-up. The first three lessons focus on learning the grid. For a later session, continue through the tutorials to use Insights, or explore the data-center activity and discuss reliability, costs, and emissions together.
