Electrify / For teachers

Grades 9–12 · 45-minute lesson

Give your class a grid to run

What keeps the lights on when the sun goes down? Let students find out by running a grid. In Electrify’s first three lessons, they watch supply and demand, build power plants, and try storing energy for later.

Before class

This lesson is for grades 9–12 in environmental science, Earth science, or engineering. Students should be able to read a line graph. By the end, they’ll use observations from the game to explain how a grid can meet demand after sunset and defend a choice about what to build.

Use one browser-capable device per pair, or project the game and let the class vote on decisions. Test Electrify on your school’s network. It’s free to play and requires no account. Choose Play. If you see saved games, choose New game. Under Learn the basics, use View all to jump to any of the first three missions.

Give each pair a student worksheet. Have one student drive and the other record evidence, then switch roles for the next mission. Preview Mission 3 before class if you plan to demonstrate storage.

Run it in one 45-minute class

0–5 min · Make a prediction

Ask whether a city with lots of solar power can meet demand after sunset. Have students write what they expect to see on the supply-and-demand chart and what evidence could change their minds.

5–13 min · Mission 1: Electricity (8 min)

Follow the prompts to open the supply-and-demand chart. Pause as daylight fades so students can note what happens to solar output, which plant is still supplying power, and how total supply compares with demand.

13–23 min · Mission 2: Generators (10 min)

In Build, have pairs compare two generators before buying. Record one listed cost or build time for each option. Ask which could help with an evening shortage and whether it would be ready in time.

23–35 min · Mission 3: Storage (12 min)

Follow the prompts to see when pumped hydro charges and discharges. Ask which generator provided the electricity to charge it and what would happen if the store ran out while demand was still high. If pairs need more time, project the storage demonstration and move on once everyone has an observation.

35–45 min · Compare plans and write an exit ticket

Compare two ways to cover evening demand, such as adding a generator or storing daytime surplus. Have each student recommend a plan, support it with two observations, and name one tradeoff. Look for evidence that relates to the choice, such as how long construction takes or when stored energy is available.

Electrify uses one representative day per month. Ask what weather or demand data students would need before applying their plan to a real town.

NGSS and AP Environmental Science alignment

Use the generator comparison to weigh the costs and benefits of different energy resources. Ask how fuel use or emissions could change a recommendation based on construction cost alone. NGSS HS-ESS3-2

Use the exit ticket to have students prioritize a criterion such as reliability or cost and explain the tradeoff between two ways to meet evening demand. NGSS HS-ETS1-3

Compare renewable solar power with nonrenewable coal, then use the chart to explain how timing affects the usefulness of each source and the role storage can play. AP Environmental Science Unit 6, Energy Resources and Consumption

Student worksheet: keep the lights on

Names ________________________ Date ______________

  1. Predict, then observe. How will a grid with lots of solar power meet demand after sunset? In Mission 1, what happens to solar output as daylight fades? Which plant still supplies power?
  2. Compare two generators. In Mission 2, name the two options you looked at. Record a cost or build time for each and explain which you’d choose for an evening shortage.
  3. Follow the stored energy. In Mission 3, where did the electricity to charge the storage come from? When did storage supply power? What would happen if it emptied too soon?
  4. Recommend a plan. Would you add a generator or store surplus power to cover the evening? Use two observations to support your choice. Name one tradeoff and one piece of data you’d need before using your plan in a real town.

Extend the lesson

For a follow-up project, choose an official scenario and investigate how extreme weather, growing demand, or an energy transition affects the same decisions. To connect utility decisions to broader climate policy, use the Electrify and En-ROADS comparison.
