Data centers and your community

Can your grid keep up?

A new data center needs power around the clock. Where will it come from, what will it cost, and who has to plan for it? Run an example grid in Electrify and see what it takes to supply that extra electricity.

Explore the impact See an example

Start with the same grid. Change one thing.

Choose a city and Electrify prepares an example grid using regional power sources, local weather, and published utility data where available. Run it once with no added data-center demand, then again with a new campus. Keep your other choices the same to see what that extra demand changes.

Without the new campus

Set the added demand to 0 MW. This keeps the grid’s existing demand, including the model’s estimate for data centers already there. Homes and businesses can still use more power as the years pass.

With the new campus

Try 100 MW of added demand. In this setup, the new campus draws that power continuously from its opening year: 2,400 MWh every day. The starting power plants stay the same, so you have to decide how to meet the extra load.

A worked example: adding 100 MW

We ran Electrify’s Austin example twice, starting in 2026 with 575,087 customer accounts. In one run, no new campus opened. In the other, a 100 MW campus opened in 2027. We left the same plants, electricity prices, and customer programs in place in both runs. Here is what happened during 2027.

Modeled result in 2027 No new campus 100 MW campus
Electricity needed No new campus14.81 TWh 100 MW campus15.68 TWh
Highest demand No new campus2,816 MW 100 MW campus2,916 MW
Smallest spare supply No new campus682 MW 100 MW campus582 MW
Fuel and running costs No new campus$448.3 million 100 MW campus$459.3 million
Emissions No new campus6.145 million tonnes CO₂e 100 MW campus6.313 million tonnes CO₂e

The grid supplied all its customers in both runs. The new campus used some of its spare supply and added about $11 million in fuel and running costs, along with 168,000 tonnes CO₂e in emissions. Try a larger campus or a different power mix and the answer may change.

These are game results recorded in October 2026, not forecasts for Austin Energy. The starting account count and electricity use draw on Austin Energy’s FY2025 figures. The plants, power output, costs, and emissions come from the simulation. Figures are rounded. A TWh is one million MWh; CO₂e expresses greenhouse gases as an equivalent amount of carbon dioxide. “Spare supply” is the smallest gap between available supply and demand during the simulation. Fuel and running costs exclude construction and financing. Game updates may change the results.

Try it yourself

  1. Open the data-center setup and select a city. Check Grid size and Assumptions to see what the example is based on. The starting plants are an illustrative fleet, not an inventory of your utility’s assets.
  2. To repeat the example above, choose Austin, set Start year to 2026 and Data centers open to 2027, and keep the grid at 575,087 accounts. Under How much extra power?, move the slider to 0 MW and choose Start exploring. Take the tour if this is your first game.
  3. In Insights, watch supply and demand, cash, and emissions. For the example above, leave the plants, prices, and customer programs unchanged and run through the end of 2027. For your own experiment, choose a date after the planned opening and write down what you see.
  4. Return to the setup. Choose the same city, grid size, and years, then set the added demand to 100 MW. Choose Start exploring to begin a separate run. Make the same decisions and compare at the same date. Any new plants or price changes need to match in both runs.
  5. Now try a different response: build generation, add storage, or fund a customer program to reduce other demand. Watch both the result and the time it takes to get there.

An annual energy total is only part of the problem. Check the hours when demand is highest or renewable output is low. A battery can help cover a gap, but it needs enough energy stored to last.

Three questions to take to your community

Will enough power be ready when the campus opens?

In the game, a plant that is still under construction cannot help with today’s shortage. Ask which generation and grid upgrades a proposal needs, when they will be ready, and what happens if they are delayed.

Who pays for the extra capacity?

Try different ways to supply the new demand and watch your costs. Then ask how the real project’s connection costs, new infrastructure, and ongoing electricity use would be paid for. The game does not calculate your household’s future bill.

What supplies the power when wind and solar are low?

Compare the emissions of your choices as well as their cost. Ask what would supply the campus on a still night, and how any promise to use clean energy accounts for those hours.

Use the model to ask better questions

Electrify is a game about energy tradeoffs. It uses one representative day per month and a simplified regional grid. It cannot predict a specific project’s reliability, connection costs, or effect on local bills. Real data centers also vary their electricity use; the constant load here makes the comparison easier to follow.
