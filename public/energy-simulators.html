Electrify: learn by running the grid

Build power plants and storage, set your electricity price, and respond when weather or growing demand upsets your plan. You watch supply, shortages, cash, and emissions as time passes. It's a free browser game you can play on a computer or phone.

Start here if you want to make the decisions and see what happens. The game overview explains what you'll manage. Teachers can use the classroom activity, and anyone curious about new electricity demand can try the data-center exercise.

Electrify uses one representative day per month. It tracks emissions, but does not calculate global temperature or establish a real grid's year-round reliability.

En-ROADS: explore the global climate picture

Adjust policies and assumptions for energy, electrification, land use, and other climate solutions. Then see how the modeled emissions and global temperature change. You can explore on your own or use it with a group and a facilitator.

Choose En-ROADS when your question spans the whole climate system. Its user guide explains the controls and the thinking behind them. Our Electrify and En-ROADS comparison looks more closely at how the two can support different parts of the same lesson.

Explore En-ROADS

Energy Transition Model: build a future energy scenario

Choose an available country, region, or municipality and a future year. Change how energy is used and supplied, try options such as storage, and see what happens to costs and emissions.

Quintel's Energy Transition Model is useful when you want to explore how the parts of an area's energy system could fit together. Check that your area is available and read the model documentation for its data sources and assumptions before drawing conclusions about a particular place.

Explore the Energy Transition Model

Energy Policy Simulator: compare policy packages

Test policies such as carbon pricing or vehicle efficiency standards across electricity, transport, buildings, industry, and other sectors. The results include emissions, costs and savings, jobs, and health effects, reported over time.

Choose Energy Innovation's Energy Policy Simulator when you want to explore the combined effects of policies in an available country or region. It is free and can be used in a browser. Its documentation explains the model's scope and provides a tutorial to get you started.

Explore the Energy Policy Simulator

Start with one question

Try asking “What happens when demand grows?” or “What changes if we build more wind power?” Change one thing, inspect the result, and note which assumptions matter. Then use another tool if you want to explore a different scale.

Read each result alongside the model’s geography, time periods, and assumptions. Two tools may give different numbers because they are answering different questions. Include that context when you share what you learn.
